How a simple plan works

You do not need to become an investing expert. The goal is to create a simple routine you can realistically maintain.

1

Choose an account

Consider a Roth IRA if you are eligible and investing for retirement. A Traditional IRA or regular brokerage account may also fit depending on your situation.

2

Automate a realistic amount

Schedule a regular transfer and invest an amount that fits your actual budget, even if it starts at $25 per month.

3

Stay hands-off

Keep contributing through normal market changes. Review once or twice each year instead of reacting to daily headlines.

Important reminders

Protect short-term money

Keep emergency savings available and address high-interest debt before investing money you may need soon.

Markets can lose value

Investments can go down, sometimes for extended periods. Returns are never guaranteed.

This is only a model

The calculator does not include taxes, fees, inflation, or the unpredictable way real markets move.

Use the calculator for planning, not promises. Focus on a contribution amount you can keep investing consistently.
Simple investing guide

A simple way to start investing

You do not need to be wealthy, an expert, or someone who watches the market every day to begin. Choose an amount you can consistently afford, automatically invest it every month into a broad fund, and give it time.

What you are trying to do

The goal is not to get rich quickly or guess what the market will do next week. The goal is to gradually build an asset that may give you more choices later in life.

The early years can look slow because your account is still small. The benefit comes from repeating the process for years and leaving the money invested. Markets rise and fall, and there will be periods when your account value is down.

The basic habit: Pick a realistic monthly amount, automate it, invest in a broad fund, reinvest dividends, and let time do the work.

What VOO is

VOO is an exchange-traded fund, commonly called an ETF. It is designed to follow the S&P 500, an index made up of large U.S. companies.

Buying VOO means you own small pieces of many large companies through one investment.

Why people use a broad fund

One fund can be easier than researching individual stocks, choosing companies, constantly trading, or managing a complicated portfolio.

Important: VOO can go down in value. Holding many companies does not prevent losses when the overall stock market falls.

What makes it work

1

Regular contributions

Add money consistently: $25, $50, $100, $150, or another amount that fits your real budget.

2

Time

The longer money stays invested, the more time it has to potentially grow.

3

Reinvestment

When dividends and growth stay invested, future returns can apply to both original deposits and past growth.

Dollar-cost averaging: Investing the same dollar amount at regular intervals rather than waiting for the “perfect” time.

What DRIP means

DRIP means Dividend Reinvestment Plan. When it is turned on, dividends automatically buy more shares instead of sitting as cash.

During working years

Many long-term investors reinvest dividends so they can continue compounding.

During retirement

Some investors turn DRIP off so dividends arrive as cash, though dividends may not cover all spending needs.

A simple setup

  1. Build a small emergency cushion first.
  2. Choose an account that fits your goal.
  3. Open the account at a major brokerage firm.
  4. Link your checking account.
  5. Pick a monthly amount you can afford in a normal month.
  6. Schedule automatic transfers and investment purchases.
  7. Turn on dividend reinvestment if you want dividends invested.
  8. Review once or twice per year.
Start with what is sustainable. The best monthly amount is the amount you can invest month after month without hurting rent, food, bills, debt payments, or emergency savings.

What happens at tax time

Tax treatment depends mainly on the account type, your income, and your filing situation.

Roth IRA

Contributions use after-tax money. Qualified retirement withdrawals can generally be tax-free if account rules are met.

Traditional IRA

Contributions may be deductible depending on income and workplace-plan coverage. Withdrawals are generally taxable.

Taxable brokerage

You can invest and withdraw without retirement-age rules, but dividends and investment sales may create tax forms.

What to do while investing

Do

  • Keep automatic investing running if your cash situation supports it.
  • Review once or twice each year.
  • Increase contributions when income rises or expenses fall.
  • Keep emergency savings separate.

Avoid

  • Watching the account every day.
  • Selling only because headlines are scary.
  • Investing money you expect to need soon.
  • Trying to trade based on short-term predictions.

What happens in retirement

Retirement shifts the focus from building the account to using it carefully. Future market returns, taxes, inflation, health costs, and the length of retirement all matter.

1

Adjust contributions

When paychecks stop, you may reduce or stop new contributions and focus on income and spending.

2

Keep near-term cash

Keep money needed soon outside stocks so you are less likely to sell after a major decline.

3

Use a planned schedule

Transfer a planned amount monthly or quarterly and review the plan each year.

Withdrawal rates are estimates, not guarantees. The monthly drawdown figure is only a first-year illustration.

The simple takeaway

Choose a realistic amount

Start with money you can afford to invest every month.

Automate it

Make the contribution happen without a new decision every month.

Give it time

Stay focused on the long-term plan through normal market changes.
