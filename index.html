How the simple plan works

1Set it up

Open a retirement account if eligible, or a regular brokerage account. Link checking and choose an amount that will not disrupt bills or emergency savings.

2Automate it

Schedule the same transfer and buy every month. This is called dollar-cost averaging: investing equal amounts on a regular schedule instead of trying to predict the market.

3Leave it working

Turn on dividend reinvestment (DRIP), avoid reacting to headlines, and review the plan once or twice each year—not every day.

What “VOO” means

VOO is an exchange-traded fund designed to track the S&P 500. Instead of trying to pick one company, it provides exposure to many large U.S. companies through one purchase. That simplicity is useful for someone who wants a hands-off approach.

DRIP on: dividends automatically buy more shares, which can compound over time. DRIP off: dividends remain cash for spending, but that cash no longer buys additional shares unless you reinvest it manually.

Taxes: simple version

Roth IRA

Contributions use after-tax money. Qualified withdrawals in retirement are generally tax-free if rules are met.

Traditional IRA

Contributions may reduce current taxable income if eligible. Withdrawals are generally taxed as ordinary income later.

Taxable brokerage

Flexible access, but dividends and realized investment gains can create annual tax reporting. The brokerage provides tax forms.

Account rules, income limits, tax treatment, and required withdrawals can change. Use official IRS guidance or a qualified tax professional for your situation.

When retirement arrives

  1. Reduce or stop auto-investing when paychecks stop.
  2. Keep a cash reserve for near-term spending rather than selling investments during every market decline.
  3. Transfer a planned monthly amount from investments to checking.
  4. Review withdrawals, taxes, and the cash reserve once or twice each year.
  5. Do not assume the portfolio will produce the same return every year; retirement withdrawals need flexibility.
